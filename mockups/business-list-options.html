The business list

Eleven rows, and on your screen ten of them say the same three words. The problem is not the spacing — it is that the row is built for a client, and a title company is not one.

What every row is telling you

The meta line reads role, stage, cadence — the three things that matter for someone you owe a call. For a business, all three are meaningless, so they collapse to the same string:

Vendor · Lead · Never touched
Vendor · Lead · Never touched
Vendor · Lead · Never touched
…ten times

The + Conv button and the tier come from the same place. You do not work biBERK on a quarterly cadence, and the MLS is not a tier B relationship you are neglecting. Meanwhile the things you actually need — what they do, the number, and who to ask for — are not on the row at all.

Option ATrade and number

Same row shape as the client list, different words. The meta line becomes what they do and who to ask for, and the + Conv button becomes the number, so the row you are looking at is the row you tap to call. Tier comes off.

Agents, vendors and partners
Smallest change, and every row now differs. Long company names still fight the number for width on a phone.
Option BGrouped by trade

Sub-headings for Title, Lending, Insurance, Brokerage, Board and MLS, Agents. The trade moves out of every row and into the heading above it, so the row carries only the name, the person, and the number.

Agents, vendors and partners
Answers “who is my title company” without reading. Six headings for eleven rows is a lot of structure for a short list.
Option COne card each

Each business gets a small card: trade above the name, preferred marked, and a footer with the number and the person who answers side by side. Room for the second number and the policy number without squeezing.

Agents, vendors and partners
The most room for what a business actually carries. Eleven cards is a longer scroll than eleven rows.

Which one

I would pick A. It is the only one that keeps the Business list looking like the Relationships list, which is the thing you asked for last time — same row, same badge, same rhythm, just words that mean something. Swapping + Conv for the phone number is the whole fix in one move: the button that did nothing useful becomes the one thing you came to the screen for.

B is genuinely better for the one question you ask most — who is my title company — and I would come back to it if this list grows past twenty. At eleven rows the headings outweigh the content. C is the one to take if you want the policy number, both of the Broker’s lines and the named contact visible without opening anything, but it breaks the row rhythm you just asked me to match across the tabs.

Whichever you pick, three things come off every business row: the tier, the stage, and the cadence state. They are client ideas, and leaving them there is what made ten rows read identically.
